## Section A 仔细阅读 Passage One
Platform companies insist that their delivery and ride-hailing apps merely connect independent workers with customers. Yet a growing body of employment research argues that the apps function as automated supervisors, exercising a degree of control that traditional managers could rarely sustain.

Algorithmic management works through constant measurement. A courier's location, speed, waiting time and customer ratings are streamed to a server that assigns orders, sets deadlines and issues penalties, often without any human being aware of the individual case. Behavioural nudges are engineered into the interface: progress bars that reset before a bonus is reached, and queues that punish logging off during peak hours. Because the system is presented as neutral mathematics, workers often find it difficult to contest a decision whose actual rulebook is a trade secret.

Defenders reply that flexibility is real: nobody is ordered to start a shift, and workers can refuse individual jobs. Researchers counter that economic pressure performs the same function as a command. When ratings determine access to future work and a single rejected order lowers the day's income, formal freedom to refuse coexists with practical compulsion. The unresolved policy question is not whether the algorithm is a manager — the evidence suggests it plainly is — but which categories of labour protection, designed for human hierarchies, should apply to automated ones.

## Section B 仔细阅读 Passage Two
As demand surges for the cobalt, nickel and rare metals used in electric-vehicle batteries, mining companies have turned their attention to the abyssal plains, four kilometres beneath the Pacific, where trillions of potato-sized mineral nodules lie scattered on the seabed. Proponents argue that land-based mining inflicts well-documented harm — deforestation, toxic tailings and, for cobalt, dangerous child labour — and that harvesting nodules would disturb no human community.

Marine biologists are unconvinced. The abyssal floor is not empty: it hosts slow-growing sponges, sea cucumbers and microbes found nowhere else, many of which take decades to mature. A collector vehicle would dredge a wide swath of sediment, killing everything in its path and discharging plumes of fine silt that smother organisms kilometres away. Because nodules themselves take millions of years to form, there is no meaningful possibility of restoration within any human timescale.

The dispute has forced a deeper question about the precautionary principle: should an activity be permitted when its long-term consequences cannot be predicted? Some regulators propose a moratorium until environmental baselines are established; industry warns that delaying metals production merely prolongs dependence on dirtier land mines. What makes the debate genuinely difficult is that both sides invoke the energy transition — one side emphasising the batteries it needs, the other the planetary systems it promises to protect.
