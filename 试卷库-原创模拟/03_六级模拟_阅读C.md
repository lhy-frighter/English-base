## Section A 仔细阅读 Passage One
The idea that sleep helps memory is ancient, but only recently have researchers understood how. During deep, slow-wave sleep, the brain replays patterns of neural activity that occurred during the day, transferring fragile new memories from the hippocampus — a temporary store — to the cortex, where they become stable and long-lasting. Interrupt this stage, and a day's studying leaves a markedly weaker trace.

Equally important is the role of rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep, when most dreaming occurs. Experiments on problem-solving suggest that REM sleep helps detect distant connections among ideas: participants who napped in REM-rich intervals solved insight puzzles far more often than those who stayed awake or napped without REM. Far from being idle downtime, sleep appears to be an active computational process that consolidates, reorganises and occasionally discards what the day deposited.

These findings carry practical implications that students routinely ignore. Cramming until dawn trades the consolidation period for a few extra hours of repetition, producing memories that feel vivid in the moment but decay within days. Spacing study across several evenings, followed by a full night's sleep, yields more durable learning than an equivalent massed session. Sleep, in this sense, is not time stolen from study; it is part of study itself.

## Section B 仔细阅读 Passage Two
When a European government recently proposed giving employees the legal right to ignore work messages outside working hours, critics called it a solution in search of a problem. White-collar workers, they argued, are already free to switch off their phones whenever they wish. Surveys of office culture suggest this freedom is more formal than real.

Expectations are communicated subtly but powerfully. A manager who emails at 11 p.m. may not demand an answer, yet the subordinate who replies at 11:05 is noticed, and the one who answers the next morning is noticed too. Messaging applications collapse the boundary between presence and availability: the green dot beside a name implies constant reachability, and read receipts turn silence into a visible act. What was once the office — a place one left at the end of the day — has become a standing obligation.

Legislation cannot rewrite culture by itself, and poorly drafted rules risk burdening genuinely flexible arrangements, such as teams spread across time zones. Nevertheless, proponents argue that a default norm matters: without an explicit right to disconnect, the burden falls on the individual to justify silence. With one, the expectation flips, and answering at midnight becomes the exception that requires explanation, rather than silence itself.
