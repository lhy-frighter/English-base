## Section A 仔细阅读 Passage One
Vertical farming — the practice of growing crops in stacked layers inside controlled indoor environments — has moved from a futuristic curiosity to a commercial reality. Advocates argue that because vertical farms use recirculating hydroponic systems, they consume up to 95 percent less water than conventional agriculture, and their proximity to city centres cuts the emissions associated with long-haul transport. Harvests are also immune to droughts, floods and the growing unpredictability of seasonal weather.

Yet the economics remain stubbornly difficult. Indoor farms require powerful LED lighting, and in northern climates electricity for lighting and climate control can account for half of operating costs. Crops such as wheat or rice, which produce relatively few calories per square metre, are effectively impossible to grow profitably indoors. As a result, commercial operators concentrate on leafy greens, herbs and soft fruits — high-value products that spoil quickly in conventional supply chains.

Critics further note that vertical farming does not so much replace land as substitute one resource for another: soil and sunlight are replaced by capital and kilowatt-hours. Where electricity comes from coal, the environmental balance sheet can look worse, not better, than a field in a sunny region. The technology, in other words, is best understood not as agriculture's universal future but as one tool among many, suited to dense cities with clean, cheap power.

## Section B 仔细阅读 Passage Two
For decades, researchers believed that raising children with two languages was a liability: sorting between competing vocabularies was thought to slow the acquisition of each. More recent evidence tells a different story. Bilingual children often command a smaller vocabulary in each individual language, yet they consistently outperform monolingual peers on tasks that require ignoring irrelevant information and switching between competing rules.

The mechanism, neuroscientists now suspect, lies in lifelong mental exercise. Every time a bilingual speaker produces a sentence, both languages remain active in the brain, and one must be suppressed while the other is selected. That constant conflict-management appears to strengthen a network of frontal brain regions involved in attention and self-control. Remarkably, the benefits extend beyond language: in laboratory conflict tasks, elderly bilinguals respond faster than monolinguals of the same age, and several studies report that dementia symptoms emerge, on average, several years later in lifelong bilinguals — even when post-mortem examination reveals comparable physical damage to the brain.

Enthusiasm should nevertheless be tempered. Much of the early dramatic evidence came from small, carefully selected samples, and large replication studies have produced smaller effects. Bilingualism is not a magic shield against cognitive decline; it is better understood as one component of a mentally stimulating life, alongside education, exercise and social engagement.

- A) an advantage in rule-switching
